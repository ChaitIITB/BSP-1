The goal and the approaches

Rank a person against peers in the same field, fairly across years

Raw citation counts favour old papers and big fields. Our idea: judge each paper by the share of its field's citations it collects soon after publication, then add the shares up per person. We compare the following approaches:

ApproachIn plain words
A1 · Hand-crafted metricThe share formula, written by us (slide 6).
A4 · Fine-tuned LLMFine-tune an LLM on ranking examples, GenRec-style (slides 14–15).
A5 · LLMs pick the best rankingAn LLM compares candidate rankings and chooses the most relevant one.

Status: Approach 1 is fully built and analysed. Approach 4 is trained and has first results (slides 14–15). Approach 5 is not built. Two further ideas — an LLM that writes the SQL, and an LLM that revises its own query and ranking rules — will be tried later.

Background: Relay-Linking (Singh et al., KDD 2017)

How citations arrive over time

  • Dating: a paper's birth year is its publication year. No real citation timestamps exist, so a citation is dated by the citing paper's birth year. The age gap of a citation is the citing paper's birth year minus the cited paper's.
  • Two forces: citations favour already-popular papers (preferential attachment) and fade as papers age (obsolescence).
  • Temporal bucket signature: for each citing year j, the share F(i, j) of citations going to papers born in year i.
  • Turnover = how fast a cohort of papers loses citations. Take citations to papers born in a year i: compare the count they get in year j with the count in j+1; the ratio is how much that cohort's citations shrink in one year. Turnover is the average ratio of j to j+1 over all cohorts i and years j. Higher turnover = older papers stop being cited sooner. It differs by community (Singh et al. report 3.97 for SIGMOD, 6.74 for ICML/NIPS).
  • Our use: the window Δ (number of years after birth in which a paper's citations count) is chosen from the age-gap distribution (slides 9–11). We have not computed the paper's turnover, distance or divergence measures, and we use years, not exact dates.

Approach 1

The field-share metric, step by step

1
For a paper born in year y, count its citations in the window [y, y+Δ], with Δ = 3.
2
Count all citations the whole set makes in the years y to y+Δ (the field total, to papers of any age).
3
Divide: this is the paper's share.
share(p) = cites(p, [y, y+Δ]) / field_total([y, y+Δ])
4
Keep papers born from 2006 up to the cutoff, 2014: the last birth year scored; later papers are ignored, as if the field had ended.
5
A person's score is the sum of their papers' shares, each split equally between co-authors. Keep people with at least 3 papers (1,430 authors).

Example: 12 citations in a window where the field makes 3,000 gives a share of 0.4%. With 4 authors, each gets 0.1%. Within one birth year the share is proportional to the citation count; the field total only rescales across years. Years, not months: a December paper gets the same window as a January one.

Definitions 2 · how rankings are compared

No independent ground truth yet, so we compare rankings with each other

Agreement, top-100 overlapFor two methods, take the authors both score. Agreement = Spearman ρ between the two score vectors. Top-100 overlap = size of the intersection of the two top-100 lists.
Tied fraction1 − (number of distinct score values) / (number of authors scored). It is the share of authors whose score repeats another author's, counting each distinct value once. 0 means all scores differ. Ties are not broken: Spearman gives tied authors their average rank, and the order inside a tie is arbitrary.
Volume influenceSpearman ρ between author score and the author's number of scored papers (born 2006–cutoff). High ρ means the ranking largely sorts by how many papers a person has, not by per-paper quality. A1 scores 0.61 on this: the metric is strongly tied to paper count (slide 9).
Noise floorRe-run Approach 1 on a random 80% of the edges, 20 times, and compare each ranking with the full-data one (Spearman ρ and top-100 overlap). The spread of those values is the noise floor: a change smaller than it is within edge-sampling noise.

New metric · verdict

It removes volume, not age bias, and costs accuracy

What it does doCuts the tie between score and paper count from 0.61 to 0.33. In the lowest scoring fifth of authors the mean paper count goes from 3.6 to 4.1, in the highest from 13.9 to 9.3: the ranking stops being a headcount.
What it does not doAge bias does not move toward zero (−0.178 → −0.185 on OpenAlex, −0.002 → +0.010 on Semantic Scholar). The bias comes from earlier papers having had longer to collect citations, which a per-author division cannot touch.
What it costsρ with future citations falls 0.083 (ten noise floors) and the top-10 mean falls from 96.5 to 22.3. Only 48 of A1's top 100 survive. Per-paper ranking rewards short records: M1's top 10 have 3–4 papers, A1's have 22–70.
The mechanismM1 and plain per-paper citations (B1) agree at ρ = 0.987. Once you divide by paper count the field-share denominator stops doing anything, so M1 is really "citations per paper", not a new field-normalized metric.
Takeaway. Per-paper and total answer different questions, and both are worth reporting: A1 for influence accumulated, M1 for typical output quality. But substituting M1 for A1 is not an improvement, and it does not fix age bias. The honest fix for age bias is in the birth window, not in the denominator.
New metric 2 · citation deflator · verdict

Correcting citation inflation does not fix age bias either

Each citation received in year t is worth D(t) = R(2021)/R(t), where R(t) is the references per paper of year t. A 2005 citation counts 1.74×, a 2021 one 1.00×.

Why a fine-tuned ranker

How the final approach would help

  • One model, many rankings. The criterion sits in the instruction: first-3-year impact, early-career only, recent work weighted more, one subfield. Hand-written SQL needs a new query for each.
  • Uses paper titles as well as citation counts, which the metric ignores.
  • Handles sparse graphs. Where edges are missing (as in Semantic Scholar), the profile text still carries signal.
  • Can write the short assessment of a person (the "review" part, my reading of that requirement, not yet confirmed), which a metric cannot.
  • Can be trained toward an independent label such as awards, not only toward our own metric.

These are intended benefits, not yet shown: the first run did not beat the paper-count baseline (slide 15). Caveat: it learns only what its labels encode. Each new criterion needs labels that reflect it, and it must be tested on held-out researchers.

Appendix · Approach 1 query

The exact query behind the field-share metric

Tables: edges(cited, citing, y_cited, y_citing) and authors(paper, year, author, n), where n is the number of authors on the paper. Δ = 3, births 2006–2014, minimum 3 papers.

CREATE TABLE a1_share AS
WITH tot AS (                       -- all in-set citations made in each year
  SELECT y_citing AS yr, COUNT(*) AS n FROM edges GROUP BY 1),
den AS (                            -- field total for the window [y, y+3]
  SELECT y.year, SUM(t.n) AS d
  FROM (SELECT DISTINCT year FROM authors WHERE year BETWEEN 2006 AND 2014) y
  JOIN tot t ON t.yr BETWEEN y.year AND y.year + 3 GROUP BY 1)
SELECT e.cited AS paper, e.y_cited AS year,
       COUNT(*) * 1.0 / ANY_VALUE(d.d) AS share
FROM edges e JOIN den d ON d.year = e.y_cited
WHERE e.y_citing <= e.y_cited + 3 AND e.y_cited BETWEEN 2006 AND 2014
GROUP BY 1, 2;

-- author score: uncited papers add 0 but still count toward the 3-paper minimum
SELECT a.author, SUM(COALESCE(s.share, 0) / a.n) AS score,
       COUNT(*) AS n_papers, SUM(COALESCE(s.c, 0)) AS raw_cites
FROM authors a LEFT JOIN a1_share s ON s.paper = a.paper
WHERE a.year BETWEEN 2006 AND 2014
GROUP BY 1 HAVING COUNT(*) >= 3;
